# SEGMENT 1 — Introduction: the marketing content bottleneck (00:00 – 05:00)

## SLIDE 1 — Can Your Business Make Its Own Images and Videos?
**⏱ 00:00 → 01:00 · 1 min · ≈ 140 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Title slide. UNESCO-ICHEI / IIOE / co-builder logos. Course title only — no imagery.

**SAY**

> Welcome. This session is called *AI for Image and Video Generation*, and it is for people who run marketing, communications, e-commerce, or training inside a business. It is not for designers, and it is not for engineers. Nothing here assumes you have ever opened a camera, a design tool, or a line of code.
>
> Before I teach anything, two questions, and I would like to see hands.
>
> **ASK** — How many of you paid an outside agency or a freelancer for marketing visuals in the last twelve months?
>
> Look around. That is most of the room.
>
> **ASK** — How many of you waited more than one week for a single revision? One change, to one image.
>
> Keep those two numbers in your head. In forty-five minutes I want to give you a method that changes both of them — and I want to be honest with you about what it does not change.

**DO:** Count the hands out loud if the room is small. On camera, name the proportion instead ("most of you").

## SLIDE 2 — The Marketing Content Bottleneck
**⏱ 01:00 → 03:00 · 2 min · ≈ 290 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Four labelled pressure blocks. Bottom line in large type: *Generation removes the production bottleneck, not the judgement bottleneck.*

**SAY**

> Let me name the problem precisely, because "artificial intelligence will help your marketing" is not a plan.
>
> There are four pressures on a small team.
>
> **One: cost per asset.** A single product photograph, lit and retouched, has a price. A ten-second video has a much larger price. Now multiply that by every market you sell into.
>
> **Two: turnaround.** The work is not slow because anybody is lazy. It is slow because a brief has to travel — from you, to an agency, to a photographer, to a retoucher, back to you, and then around the whole loop again for one revision.
>
> **Three: the volume of variants.** The same product needs a square crop for one platform, a vertical crop for another, a different language overlay for each market, and a seasonal version twice a year. The creative idea finished months ago. Only the variants remain. This is the work that drains teams the most — and it is the work that generation handles best.
>
> **Four: no designer.** Many of you have nobody in the building who can make an image at all.
>
> Now the promise, and the limit. I will say this once at the start of the session and once at the end.
>
> Generative AI removes the **production** bottleneck. It does not remove the **judgement** bottleneck.
>
> Making the picture becomes cheap. Deciding whether the picture is true, whether it is on brand, whether you are allowed to publish it, and whether it will mislead a customer — none of that becomes cheaper. Some of it becomes more expensive, because you can now produce a hundred bad decisions in a single afternoon.
>
> So this session has two halves. The first half is **method**: how to make the model do what you want. The second half is **judgement**: how not to hurt yourself with it.

**IF ASKED — "Which tools should we buy?"** → *This course teaches capability categories, not products. Tools change every six months. The method does not. Appendix F of your course material gives you five criteria to choose a tool yourself — and the first criterion is commercial-use licensing.*

## SLIDE 3 — Two Capabilities: Text-to-Image and Image-to-Video
**⏱ 03:00 → 05:00 · 2 min · ≈ 265 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Two boxes. Left: *words → still image*. Right: *still image + words → moving shot*. A third box, greyed out: *words → moving shot (not your default)*.

**SAY**

> First, four terms we will use for the rest of the session. Please write them down.
>
> **Generative AI** is a system that produces new text, images, audio, or video from a written instruction.
>
> A **prompt** is that written instruction. It is the only channel you have. There is no other way to talk to the model.
>
> **Text-to-image** means: you give words, the model returns a still picture.
>
> **Image-to-video** means: you give a still picture *and* words, and the model returns a moving shot. The picture fixes what is in the frame. The words say what happens to it.
>
> There is a third capability you will see advertised everywhere: **text-to-video** — words alone, straight to a moving shot. I am not going to teach it as your default, and I want to explain why, because this is the single most common disappointment in the whole area.
>
> With text-to-video, the model invents the subject *and* the movement at the same time, on every single attempt. So the product looks slightly different each time. For an artist, that variety is a feature. For a business that must show the same package, on the same shelf, across the same campaign, it is a defect.
>
> Image-to-video separates the two problems. You decide the subject once, you approve it, and then you generate movement from that approved picture. The subject stops drifting.
>
> So: two capabilities. **Text-to-image to decide what the thing looks like. Image-to-video to make it move.** Everything else today is detail.
>
> Remember that ordering, because at minute thirty-five I will run an entire workflow built on exactly those two steps.

**DO:** Point at the greyed-out third box when you dismiss it. The visual and the sentence must land together.

# SEGMENT 2 — How generation works, and what the model cannot infer (05:00 – 10:00)

## SLIDE 4 — The Model Does Not Guess
**⏱ 05:00 → 07:00 · 2 min · ≈ 185 words + DEMO BEAT 1**
**ON SCREEN:** Split screen — the prompt text on the left, four generated results on the right.

**DEMO BEAT 1** — *Have the four generations already rendered. Do not wait on a live model here; the pause kills the segment. Keep a live generation for slide 5 only.*

**SAY**

> I want to prove something to you before I teach you anything.
>
> Here is a brief. Read it with me.
>
> *"We are a small business that sells hand-woven textiles. We need one product image for the export catalogue, and one eight-second video for social media. We have no photographer, no studio, and no designer."*
>
> That is a real brief, from a real kind of business. Now watch what a normal first attempt looks like.
>
> **DO** — *Reveal the prompt.* I am going to type the prompt that most people type: a short, reasonable sentence. And I am going to generate it four times, without changing a single character.
>
> **DO** — *Reveal the four results one at a time. Let each sit for two beats.*
>
> Four results. Same words. Different camera angle, every time. Different light. Different weave in the fabric. In two of them, the product is a different colour.
>
> Nobody made a mistake here. Nothing is broken. The tool worked exactly as designed.
>
> So here is the rule, and it is the first of my three messages today.
>
> **The model does not guess.** Anything you leave out of the prompt, the model decides at random. An unspecified dimension is not a neutral default — it is a **delegation**. You handed that decision to a machine, silently, and you did not notice that you had done it.

**TRIM:** If behind, cut the "Nobody made a mistake" paragraph. The rule still lands.

## SLIDE 5 — A Prompt Is a Shooting Script, Not a Wish
**⏱ 07:00 → 10:00 · 3 min · ≈ 370 words + DEMO BEAT 2**
**ON SCREEN:** Two columns. Left, struck through: *wish words*. Right: *filmable facts*. Four conversion pairs.

**DEMO BEAT 2** — *This is the one live generation of the first half. Have the fallback screen-recording cued; if the tool is slow or rate-limited, switch to the recording without comment.*

**CLIPS** — *Below the slide: a ten-second extract, a dolly out at sunset, displayed with its fully specified English prompt of over six hundred characters — duration, movement, character performance, background, light, style. Start it as you say "every part of it that is good, is good because I specified it": on a subject other than the textile brief, it is the proof that a fully written prompt gives a fully directed result. A second panel, with no video, sets an intent prompt — show that he is nervous — against its filmable version, a close-up on fingers rubbing the edge of a phone. It repeats the pair you have just revealed on screen; leave it up for the whole conversion exercise.*

**SAY**

> So let us fix it. And the fix is not a magic word. It is a change in what *kind* of sentence you write.
>
> Most people write a **wish**. "An elegant, high-quality textile product image with a warm, premium atmosphere." That is a wish. It describes the feeling you want the customer to have. The problem is that a camera cannot film *elegant*. A camera cannot film *premium*. Those words have no pixels.
>
> What the model needs is a **shooting script** — a description of what the camera sees, and what the camera does.
>
> Here is the test I want you to take away. It is one question, and it works on every prompt you will ever write:
>
> **For every adjective, ask — how would a camera capture this?**
>
> If you cannot answer, the adjective is not an instruction. Replace it with an action, a light position, or a composition.
>
> **DO** — *Reveal each pair as you say it. Do not read the left column in a flat voice; read it the way a client writes it.*
>
> "Nervous." How does a camera film nervous? → *He rubs his cuff repeatedly with his thumb. His eyes move to the door three times, and back.*
>
> "Sad." → *She lowers her head. Her eyelashes tremble twice. One drop of water slides along her jawline.*
>
> "An oppressive atmosphere." → *Top light. The eye sockets fall into shadow. The edges of the frame are underexposed. The subject does not move.*
>
> "Premium quality" — this is the business one. → *Soft lateral light. Shallow depth of field. Matte finish. A slow push-in. Empty space around the product.*
>
> "Trustworthy and friendly." → *Eye-level camera. Frontal soft light. The subject faces the camera and does not move away.*
>
> Notice what happened in every single case. The adjective disappeared, and a physical, filmable fact took its place. The camera now has something to do.
>
> Let me rewrite our textile brief as a shooting script, and generate it once.
>
> **DO** — *Live generation. Narrate while it renders: name each of the six elements as it appears in the prompt on screen.*
>
> One attempt. Correct angle. Correct light. The weave is consistent.
>
> It is not perfect. But it is **directed**. And that is the difference. Every part of it that is good, is good because I specified it — and every part that is still wrong, I can now *name*, and therefore fix. That is what a wish cannot give you: a wish gives you no handle to debug with. A script does.
>
> Second message of the day: **a prompt is a shooting script, not a wish.**

**IF ASKED — "Does this mean longer prompts are always better?"** → *No. It means more **specified dimensions**, which is not the same thing. Twenty precise words beat two hundred decorative ones. Adjectives that a camera cannot film actively hurt you, because they consume the model's attention and produce nothing.*

# SEGMENT 3 — The six-element prompt (10:00 – 19:00)

## SLIDE 6 — The Six-Element Formula
**⏱ 10:00 → 13:00 · 3 min · ≈ 350 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Six-row table — element, what to write, consequence if omitted. The "consequence" column highlighted.

**CLIPS** — *Below the slide: an eight-second extract, a slow dolly in down a Tokyo street after the rain, whose prompt is written in the six lines of the formula — Subject · Action · Setting · Camera · Style · Audio. Start it as you reach element four. The room watches the formula turn into a picture while you read it out, and the prompt itself is the worked example of the table: it is the shortest proof you can give.*

**SAY**

> Now I will give you the structure. Six elements. This is the entire method, and it is transferable — it does not belong to any one tool, and it will still be true when you change tools next year.
>
> **One: Subject** — who or what is in the frame. Age, appearance, clothing, material, finish.
>
> **Two: Action** — what they are doing. A specific physical action, plus a speed adverb.
>
> **Three: Setting** — where and when. Place, time of day, weather, key props.
>
> **Four: Camera movement** — how the virtual camera moves. The movement type, *plus the shot size it starts on and the shot size it ends on.*
>
> **Five: Style** — the look. Colour grade, contrast, film character, depth of field.
>
> **Six: Audio** — what we hear. Ambient sound, effects, score, or deliberately no score.
>
> I could now tell you what to write in each box. That is the least useful way to teach this. Instead I will tell you what happens when you leave each one out — because that is what you will actually recognise from your own attempts.
>
> **DO** — *Reveal the consequence column one row at a time.*
>
> Leave out the **subject**, and the subject looks different in every generation. You cannot build a campaign on that.
>
> Leave out the **action**, and the frame is static — or the movement is uncontrolled, and the model invents one for you.
>
> Leave out the **setting**, and you get a random background that does not belong to your subject. This is where the floating, placeless look comes from.
>
> Leave out **camera movement**, and you get a locked-off camera. Flat. Lifeless. It looks like a security recording of a nice object.
>
> Leave out **style**, and you get the default grade — and the default grade is the generic look that everybody can now recognise in under a second, and that reads as cheap.
>
> Leave out **audio**, and you get silence. Or worse: sound that does not match the picture.
>
> Elements four and five are the ones almost everybody omits. They are also the two that carry the most visual quality per word. So if you remember nothing else from this slide, remember this: **camera and style are where the money is.**

**DO:** Ask the room to check the prompt still on screen from slide 5, and count the six elements in it. Ten seconds, no discussion.

## SLIDE 7 — Elements 1–3: Subject, Action, Setting
**⏱ 13:00 → 16:00 · 3 min · ≈ 415 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Three panels. The reference-photography rule set in large type. Lower half: the model prompt, with the six elements colour-coded.

**SAY**

> Let us take the first three elements into a real business case, because the details are where the money is.
>
> **Subject.** Not "a textile". Say what it is made of, how old it looks, what colour it actually is, what the finish is, and give the viewer a sense of scale. *"A hand-woven wool blanket, undyed cream with two indigo stripes, visible irregular weave, folded once, roughly the size of an adult's arms full."*
>
> Now the business rule for subject. It is the most important sentence on this slide.
>
> **If the product must look exactly like the real object, do not describe it in words. Supply a real photograph as reference.**
>
> Words produce *a similar product*. They never produce *your product*. A described weave is a weave the model has seen somewhere before. Your weave is yours. So for anything that must match physical reality — the product, the packaging, the label — you upload a photograph, and you let the model condition on it.
>
> Hold that thought. In three slides I will turn it into a formal rule with three levels.
>
> **Action.** Give a physical process, and give it a speed. Not "flowing fabric". *"The blanket is lifted at one corner by a hand, and falls back slowly under its own weight."* Now the model knows what changes between the first frame and the last — and how fast it changes.
>
> **Setting.** Place, time of day, weather, and props. And one warning: **props are where cultural mistakes happen.** If you set a scene in a market, a workshop, or a home, you are asserting facts about a place and a people. Only assert what you can verify. We will come back to this in the ethics discussion.
>
> Here is the model example I will keep returning to all session. It is not a business example — it is an artistic one — and I keep it because every technique in this course lands somewhere inside it.
>
> **DO** — *Read the prompt at full speed, without commentary. Let the density speak.*
>
> *"A fisherman in his sixties from the Brittany coast. Short grey-white hair. Dark oilskin jacket. Hands marked with salt and cracks. He slowly draws in a fishing net; the rope slides between his fingers; water drips from the back of his hand.*
>
> *Setting: a granite quay at five in the morning, thin mist not yet lifted, a lighthouse beam still sweeping the sea in the distance, cables and buoys scattered at his feet.*
>
> *Camera: slow lateral track, from a medium shot to a close-up of the hands, ending on the point where the knuckles meet the rope.*
>
> *Style: documentary cinema, cold grey-blue dominant, low colour-temperature morning light, shallow depth of field, 35mm film grain.*
>
> *Audio: low-frequency surf, the creak of a loaded cable, distant gulls. No score."*
>
> Count the elements. All six are there. That is why it works — and that is why it will still work in three years, on a tool that does not exist yet.

**TRIM:** If behind, cut the "props are where cultural mistakes happen" paragraph here and cover it in slide 16 instead.

## SLIDE 8 — Elements 4–6: Camera, Style, Audio — Where the "AI Look" Comes From
**⏱ 16:00 → 19:00 · 3 min · ≈ 430 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Three panels. Style panel shows four labelled dials: colour temperature · contrast · grain · depth of field. Bottom banner: *Never let the model draw your logo.*

**CLIPS** — *Below the slide: four fifteen-second extracts, the longest in the course, shot on the same framing and differing only by style — warm tones · cool tones · high saturation · low saturation. Run them one after the other as you say "that is a style. It is executable — and, more importantly for a business, it is repeatable". Four prompts, one setting changed each time: it is the cleanest demonstration you have of what "executable" and "repeatable" mean together. Because the extracts are silent, element six — audio — is not illustrated here; say so, do not leave the room to guess.*

**SAY**

> Now the three elements people skip.
>
> **Camera movement.** I am not going to make you memorise ten movements in three minutes — they are on your reference card. I will give you the two rules that prevent almost every failure.
>
> Rule one: **always write the start and the end shot size.** "Dolly in" is not executable. It does not say where you begin or where you stop, so the model picks for you. *"Push from a full shot of the workshop to a close-up of the hands at the loom"* — that is executable.
>
> Rule two: **one movement per shot.** When people stack three or four movements into a single prompt, the model executes the first one — or blurs all of them together. If you want three movements, you want three shots.
>
> **Style.** This is where the generic look lives. And the word *"cinematic"* is not a style. It is a mood you have about a style. The model cannot execute it, so it falls back on its default: over-smooth skin, over-saturated colour, contrast that is too high, no grain, and light coming from nowhere. Everybody now recognises that look in under a second, and it reads as cheap.
>
> So write style in four separate parts.
>
> **Colour temperature** — warm, cold, or mixed. **Contrast** — high or low. **Grain or texture** — 35mm grain, clean digital, or matte. **Depth of field** — shallow, or deep and sharp.
>
> *"Cold grey-blue dominant, low colour-temperature morning light, shallow depth of field, 35mm film grain."* That is a style. It is executable — and, more importantly for a business, it is **repeatable**.
>
> **Audio.** Almost everybody forgets audio on the first attempt. Write it in three layers: ambient sound, specific effects, and score — or write *"no score"*, which is a legitimate and often better choice. Silence with one real sound in it beats generic music every time.
>
> And now one enterprise warning, before we move on. Please write this one down.
>
> **Image models render text, logos, and fine typography unreliably.** They get letters wrong. They invent extra words. They approximate a logo that is almost right, which is worse than obviously wrong.
>
> So: **never let the model draw your logo, your packaging text, or your legal copy.** Generate the picture. Then put real vector artwork and real type on top, in a normal design tool, afterwards.
>
> This one habit will save you from most of the embarrassments in this entire area — and it costs you nothing, because it is only a change in the order you work.

**IF ASKED — "Has any tool got text right yet?"** → *Rendering of short text keeps improving, and some tools are much better than others. Do not build a workflow on the current best case. Keep the composite-afterwards step; it is correct regardless of how good the model becomes, because it also gives you editable, accessible, legally reviewable type.*

# SEGMENT 4 — Fidelity, light, and framing (19:00 – 26:00)

## SLIDE 9 — Three Fidelity Tiers
**⏱ 19:00 → 22:00 · 3 min · ≈ 380 words + DEMO BEAT 3**
**ON SCREEN:** Three-tier table with a rule under each tier. Then a side-by-side: generated catalogue image | real product photograph.

**DEMO BEAT 3** — *Have both images pre-rendered at the same display size. The comparison must be instant; searching for a file destroys the moment.*

**SAY**

> This is the most important slide of the session for a business. If you forget the six elements, you will make an ugly picture. If you forget this, you will make an expensive mistake.
>
> Not every image has to be true to the same degree. So before you write a single prompt, sort the asset into one of three tiers.
>
> **Tier 1 — must be literally true.** The product itself. The packaging. The label. The ingredient list. The face of a real employee. Anything a customer could reasonably rely on, or compare against what arrives in the post.
>
> The Tier 1 rule: use real photography as your reference, generate as little as you can, and **verify the output against the physical object** before it is published.
>
> **Tier 2 — illustrative.** Lifestyle scenes. Mood. Backgrounds. Concepts. A hand holding a cup, a street, a landscape, a texture. Nothing in a Tier 2 asset makes a factual claim about your product.
>
> The Tier 2 rule: generate freely. This is where the productivity gain actually lives, and most of your volume is Tier 2.
>
> **Tier 3 — a fictional person.** A spokesperson who does not exist. A "customer" in your video who is not a customer.
>
> The Tier 3 rule: consent and disclosure obligations apply. We come to them in twenty minutes.
>
> **DO** — *Reveal the side-by-side. Do not narrate it. Wait.*
>
> **ASK** — Look at the screen. This is the catalogue image we generated earlier. Beside it is a photograph of the real product. Same weave?
>
> …No.
>
> **ASK** — Same colour under daylight?
>
> …Close. Not the same.
>
> **ASK** — Same dimensions?
>
> …We cannot tell. And neither can the customer — until the parcel arrives.
>
> This is a Tier 1 asset. And what you have just seen is the single most common way a small business harms itself with generative AI: **it treats a Tier 1 asset as if it were Tier 2.**
>
> The image looks beautiful. The customer receives something different. That is not a technical problem. That is a consumer-protection problem — and in most jurisdictions, it is also a legal one.
>
> The whole discipline is one question, asked before you start: **which tier is this asset?** The answer changes your workflow, your budget, your review step, and your legal exposure. Ask it first, every time.

## SLIDE 10 — Light: Direction, Quality, and Colour
**⏱ 22:00 → 24:00 · 2 min · ≈ 300 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Three labelled strips — direction · quality · time of day. "Volumetric lighting" set apart visually.

**CLIPS** — *Below the slide, three groups of ten five-second extracts, one per strip: the light source (moonlight · overcast light) · direction and quality (soft light · top light · back light · under light) · the time of day (daylight · night · sunrise · sunset — magic hour). Ten prompts for ten settings, one word of light changed each time. Scroll a group during the sentence that concerns it. If you apply the late-running plan and cut the time-of-day paragraph, leave the group displayed: it costs no seconds of narration and still carries the point.*

**SAY**

> Two minutes on light, because it is the cheapest quality you can buy with words.
>
> Light has **direction.** Frontal light gives clear detail and almost no depth — it is flat, and it suits a product display or an identification-style portrait. Lateral light gives half light and half shade, and that is where texture and volume come from — this is your craftsmanship shot, your material close-up. Backlight separates the subject from the background with a bright outline, underexposes the face, and is strongly atmospheric — it belongs in an aspirational brand film, usually as the closing shot. Top light drops the eye sockets into shadow and reads as oppressive. Under-lighting looks wrong to every human eye on earth. Avoid both in marketing unless you mean it.
>
> Light has **quality.** Hard light — sharp shadow edges, high contrast, dramatic. Soft light — graduated shadows, forgiving, and the right default for people and for products. And **volumetric** light — visible beams, where the air itself carries mist, dust, or smoke.
>
> Let me give you a trade secret. Of every word in this session, **"volumetric lighting" has the highest value per word.** One phrase, and the perceived production quality of your image jumps noticeably. Use it deliberately, and do not overuse it.
>
> Light has **time.** Golden hour — warm, low angle, long shadows. Blue hour — cold blue, a detailed sky, underexposed ground. Solar noon — hard shadows from above, flattened saturation, and the reason professional photographers do not shoot at noon. Urban night — mixed colour temperatures, which is difficult and beautiful.
>
> And **colour.** Write three separate instructions: the dominant colour, the contrast, and the saturation.
>
> Do not write "a high-end feel". Write *"warm dominant, low contrast, desaturated."* One is a mood. The other is an instruction.

**TRIM:** If behind schedule, cut the time-of-day paragraph entirely. It is on the reference card.

## SLIDE 11 — Shot Size and Composition
**⏱ 24:00 → 26:00 · 2 min · ≈ 240 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Six frames in a row, widest to tightest, each captioned with its business use. Below: five composition terms.

**CLIPS** — *Below the slide, three groups of twelve extracts of about five seconds: composing the frame (centred composition · symmetrical composition · balanced framing · off-centre subject) · shot size (close-up · medium shot · full shot · wide shot) · shot types and points of view (single shot · two shot · group shot · establishing shot). The middle group illustrates four of the six sizes you have just named — extreme wide and extreme close-up have no extract; do not promise all six. The third group counts the subjects in frame, and it does not illustrate the three shot types you name either — point of view, over-the-shoulder and the insert have no extract. Present it as a complement, not as the picture of that paragraph. The composition group shows the default centring beside the off-centre subject: it is the visual proof of your rule, "composition words must be written into the prompt." No group costs a second of narration, so if you apply the late-running plan, leave all three displayed.*

**SAY**

> Shot size is not decoration. Shot size decides what this shot is *for*.
>
> From the widest. **Extreme wide** — the scale of the operation, the site, the geography. **Wide** — establish the scene. **Full** — the whole product, or the whole person in action. **Medium** — the workhorse; this is where demonstration and conversation live, and it will be most of your shots. **Close-up** — reaction, quality, detail. **Extreme close-up** — craftsmanship, texture, the one decisive object.
>
> Three more worth knowing. **Point of view** — the viewer becomes the character; the strongest identification tool you have. **Over-the-shoulder** — the standard for two people talking. And **the insert** — a cutaway to an object, which controls pacing and controls *when* the audience learns something.
>
> Composition: rule of thirds, symmetry, leading lines, foreground occlusion, and negative space.
>
> One rule, and it is the same rule as everything else today: **composition words must be written into the prompt.** The model does not follow composition by default. If you want the subject on the left third, say so.
>
> And one business addition that designers already know and that marketers usually miss: **if the asset is for advertising, reserve negative space for the text.** Say it in the prompt — *"empty space in the upper third"* — because if you do not, there will be nowhere to put your headline, and you will regenerate the entire thing.

# SEGMENT 5 — Camera movement and multi-shot video (26:00 – 35:00)

## SLIDE 12 — Camera Movement: Name It, Give It a Start and an End
**⏱ 26:00 → 29:00 · 3 min · ≈ 340 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Ten movements in a two-column list, each with its narrative function in four words or fewer.

**CLIPS** — *Below the slide, three groups of twelve extracts: dolly and tracking moves (dolly in · dolly out · tracking shot · follow shot) · crane, orbit and pans (orbit shot · crane down · pan · tilt) · handheld, drone, whip pan and rack focus (handheld · aerial / drone · whip pan · rack focus). Each of the ten movements you name has its own extract — the last two, follow shot and rack focus, are not on your list: present them as two additions, not as two omissions. Use them for the reversal exercise: at each **ASK**, let the room answer, then point to the matching extract instead of repeating the answer.*

**SAY**

> We have now moved from still images to video. In video, camera movement does the most work — and it is the element you cannot fake.
>
> Here is the vocabulary. Ten movements. You will not memorise them in three minutes, and you do not need to — they are on your card. But you should be able to *recognise* them, because naming a movement precisely is exactly what makes it executable.
>
> **Dolly in** — the camera physically approaches. It concentrates attention and enters the subject's inner life.
>
> **Dolly out** — the camera withdraws. It reveals the environment, and it creates isolation or scale.
>
> **Pan** — the camera turns horizontally on its axis. It moves attention and establishes where things are relative to each other.
>
> **Tilt** — the same, vertically. It shows height, and it takes us from a part to the whole.
>
> **Tracking** — the camera travels alongside the subject. Energy, and identification.
>
> **Orbit** — the camera circles. It says: *this subject is worth walking around.* It suspends time.
>
> **Crane** — the viewpoint rises, from a person to a landscape. Epic scale.
>
> **Handheld** — the frame breathes. Tension, documentary truth, a loss of control.
>
> **Aerial** — geography, and comparison of scale.
>
> **Whip pan** — a fast horizontal blur. It is a transition and a rhythm change at the same time.
>
> Now let us do this backwards, which is how you will actually use it in practice. I will give you an emotion. You tell me the movement. Do not look for a right answer — look for the one that *expresses* it.
>
> **ASK** — "Relief." …
>
> A slow dolly out. The frame opens, the character gets smaller, the world gets bigger.
>
> **ASK** — "Being watched." …
>
> A very slow push-in that never resolves. The audience feels the approach without ever seeing what is approaching.
>
> **ASK** — "Pride in craft." …
>
> An orbit. Because an orbit says: this object is worth walking around.
>
> **ASK** — "A long day ending." …
>
> A crane, or a locked-off wide with the light changing. Both are correct.
>
> Notice what happened in every case. You did not choose a movement because it looked impressive. You chose it because it *meant* something. That is the third rule of camera work, and it is the one that separates a competent video from a showreel: **movement serves narrative intent.** If a movement does not mean anything, cut it and hold the shot.

**TRIM:** If behind, ask only the first two emotions.

## SLIDE 13 — Multi-Shot Scripts: Three Ways to Segment
**⏱ 29:00 → 32:00 · 3 min · ≈ 370 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Three columns — A / B / C — each with a sample line and its cost. Column C visually dominant.

**CLIPS** — *Below the slide, two groups. The first is written, with no video: the three real prompts, one per method — natural-language segments, timecodes, style anchor — displayed in full so that the room can see the difference in form. Leave that group on screen through your whole explanation of the three methods. Note that the anchor displayed is a warmer one than the cold grey-blue example you read aloud; both are valid, and the point of the panel is the form, not the palette. The second group is the seven cuts in seven extracts: shot and reverse shot · follow then reveal · vertical descent into the interior · reflection transition · whip pan · storytelling by rack focus · time ellipsis through cuts. Seven extracts, seven prompts, not one word spoken: they run under your voice.*

**SAY**

> A brand video is never one shot. It is a sequence. So how do you write several shots inside one prompt? There are three methods, and each one has a real cost. I am going to give you the cost, not just the technique, because choosing between them is a judgement call.
>
> **Method A — natural-language segments.** You write: *"The shot opens on the loom. Then the camera moves to the hands. Then we see the finished blanket, folded."*
>
> It is the easiest to write, the easiest for a beginner, and it suits two to four shots.
>
> The cost: **shot boundaries are unstable.** The model may merge your three shots into one continuous move. Or it may cut where you never asked for a cut.
>
> **Method B — timestamps.** You write: *"Zero to three seconds: wide shot of the workshop. Three to six: close-up of the hands. Six to eight: the finished product."*
>
> The boundaries are now unambiguous. This is the clearest method, and it suits rhythm-driven advertising, where the cut has to land on the beat.
>
> The cost: **hard-coded time makes the model rush.** If you give an action two seconds and it needs four, the model does not fail — it *compresses*. The movement looks accelerated and artificial, and you will not understand why, because the prompt was perfectly clear.
>
> **Method C — the style anchor.** This is not really a segmentation method at all. It is the thing that makes segmentation survivable.
>
> You write one global style declaration, and it applies to every shot in the sequence. Something like:
>
> **DO** — *Reveal the anchor text in large type. Read it slowly.*
>
> *"All shots: documentary cinema, cold grey-blue dominant, low colour-temperature morning light, shallow depth of field, 35mm film grain, no score."*
>
> Then you write your individual shots underneath it.
>
> Method C is the core technique of this entire module, and I want to explain why, because it is not obvious.
>
> When people make a multi-shot video, the failure they complain about is almost never "shot two is ugly". It is **"shot two looks like a different film from shot one."** Warm grain in the first shot. Cold, clean, digital in the second. Same product, same day, same story — and the audience feels the seam, even when they cannot name it.
>
> **Style drift is the real enemy of multi-shot work.** Not bad shots. Inconsistent ones.
>
> The style anchor is the countermeasure. Write it once, at the top, and reuse it verbatim across the entire campaign.

## SLIDE 14 — Brand Consistency: The Style Anchor
**⏱ 32:00 → 35:00 · 3 min · ≈ 390 words + DEMO BEAT 4**
**ON SCREEN:** Three levers, numbered. Then the demonstration: broken prompt → drifted shots; restored prompt → coherent shots.

**DEMO BEAT 4** — *Pre-render both versions. The value of this beat is the contrast, not the generation.*

**SAY**

> Three more levers for consistency. They are small, and they matter more than anything else in this session.
>
> **Lever one: repeat the subject description verbatim.** Not approximately — *verbatim*. "A sixty-year-old fisherman" and "the old fisherman" are, to the model, two different people. It has no memory of your last prompt. So synonyms are not elegance here. Synonyms are drift. Copy and paste your subject sentence into every single shot.
>
> **Lever two: use image-to-video, not text-to-video.** Fix the character or the product once, with a text-to-image still. Approve that still. Then generate every shot from it. This is the single largest consistency improvement available to you — and it costs nothing.
>
> **Lever three: name the key props in every shot.** If the story depends on an object — the blanket, the tool, the cup, the box — mention it explicitly in every shot, even when it feels redundant. *Especially* when it feels redundant.
>
> Now let me break all three rules on purpose.
>
> **DO** — *Show the working prompt. Edit it on screen: change "the textile" to "the fabric". Delete the style anchor line. Generate.*
>
> Look at the third shot. Different colour temperature. Different grain. And the product now has a different weave from the first shot.
>
> **DO** — *Restore both. Generate again.*
>
> Same film. Same product. Same campaign.
>
> That failure was more instructive than any success I could have shown you — because this is exactly what happens in real teams. Somebody rewrites a prompt to make it "nicer". Somebody tidies the wording. And the campaign silently loses its coherence, and nobody notices until the assets are side by side.
>
> Now the business point, which is why this slide matters more than the technique on it.
>
> **The style anchor is where your brand lives.** Brand colour. Lens character. Grade. Grain. You write them once, as a style anchor, and you reuse them across every asset in the campaign — and next year, and for the next product, and for the product after that.
>
> At that point it is no longer a prompt trick. It is a **brand asset**, stored in a file, that anyone on your team can pick up and use.
>
> That is the moment prompting stops being a personal skill and starts being an organisational capability. Hold that thought — it is the whole of slide seventeen.

# SEGMENT 6 — Workflow, rights, and disclosure (35:00 – 43:00)

## SLIDE 15 — The Default Business Workflow
**⏱ 35:00 → 38:00 · 3 min · ≈ 360 words + DEMO BEAT 5**
**ON SCREEN:** The nine-step workflow diagram, revealed step by step. Then the demonstration, full screen.

**DEMO BEAT 5** — *Run the chain end to end: text-to-image from a real reference photograph → approve one still → three image-to-video shots from that still under one style anchor → assemble into eight seconds. Use the offline recording if the live tool is slow.*

**SAY**

> Here is the entire method on one slide. This is the default business workflow, and it is exactly what I am about to run on screen.
>
> **One: the brief.** Not "what image do we want" — **"what must this asset do?"** Who sees it, where, and what should they do afterwards?
>
> **Two: the fidelity tier check.** Tier 1, 2, or 3. This decides whether you need a real photograph in hand before you begin.
>
> **Three: capability selection.** Text-to-image to decide the subject. Image-to-video to make it move.
>
> **Four: the prompt.** Six elements — subject, action, setting, camera movement, style, audio.
>
> **Five: the reference lock.** An approved subject still, plus your brand style anchor. Written once, reused everywhere.
>
> **Six: generate short shots.** Five to ten seconds each.
>
> **Seven: human review.** Brand fit. Factual accuracy against the real product. Artefacts. Representation. Rights.
>
> **Eight: provenance mark and disclosure label**, for the platform you are actually publishing on.
>
> **Nine: publish — and store the prompt in the team library.**
>
> Let me run it.
>
> **DO** — *Demonstration. Narrate the length of each clip out loud as it appears.*
>
> Watch the length of each clip. About five seconds. About eight. Six.
>
> That is not the tool failing. That is the current reality of this technology, and I want you to hear it plainly, because managing this one expectation will save you a great deal of frustration next week.
>
> **Most tools generate roughly five to ten seconds at a time, and consistency degrades as a clip gets longer.**
>
> So a brand film is not *generated*. A brand film is **assembled** — from many short shots, held together by one style anchor, and cut together in a normal editor.
>
> If you go back to your office and try to produce a sixty-second film from a single prompt, you will fail. And you will conclude that the technology does not work.
>
> It does work. It works at eight seconds at a time. Plan for eight seconds, and you will look like a genius. Plan for sixty, and you will look like a beginner.

**IF ASKED — "Will the clips get longer?"** → *Yes, and quickly. But plan your workflow around the constraint you have today, and keep the assembly step. Assembly is good practice even when clips are long, because it is the only place a human being reviews what the model actually produced.*

## SLIDE 16 — Rights, Disclosure, and Trust
**⏱ 38:00 → 41:00 · 3 min · ≈ 390 words + DEMO BEAT 6**
**ON SCREEN:** Four numbered obligations. A separate small box: *provenance mark ≠ disclosure label*. Dates shown as text only — no flags, no maps.

**DEMO BEAT 6** — *Return to the finished eight-second asset from slide 15 and leave it on screen while the disclosure question is put to the room.*

**SAY**

> Third message of the day: **for business, one impressive image is not the deliverable.** Repeatable consistency, factual fidelity, cleared rights, and honest disclosure — that is the deliverable. A beautiful asset you cannot reproduce, or that misrepresents your product, is a liability.
>
> Four obligations. And one disclaimer first, which I will not skip: **this is not legal advice.** I am a trainer, not your lawyer. Regulation in this area is moving quickly and it differs by jurisdiction. What I am teaching you is the *habit of checking*.
>
> **One: disclosure.** In the European Union, Article 50 of the Artificial Intelligence Act applies from the second of August 2026. Deep fakes — synthetic or manipulated image, audio, or video that would falsely appear to a person to be authentic — must be disclosed to the viewer, at first exposure.
>
> And here is the detail that catches people out. An embedded, machine-readable mark **alone does not discharge that duty.** A **provenance mark** is a technical signal inside the file. A **disclosure label** is a message a human being can read. They are different objects, doing different jobs, and you may well need both. For systems already placed on the market before that date, the machine-readable marking duty under Article 50(2) applies from the second of December 2026.
>
> **Two: platform labels.** Completely independently of any law, the major advertising and social platforms each run their own artificial-intelligence labelling rule — and they are not the same rule. Check the one you actually publish on. Every time.
>
> **Three: substantiation.** Generating an image does not relax advertising law. Your claims still need evidence, and a generated image must not materially mislead a consumer about the product. This is exactly the Tier 1 problem from slide nine — now with a regulator attached to it.
>
> **Four: likeness and data.** Get consent before you generate the face of a real, identifiable person. Some jurisdictions now impose specific advertising disclosure duties for synthetic performers.
>
> And the one businesses most often get wrong: **do not upload customer personal data, employee records, or unreleased product designs to a tool whose terms of service and data-retention policy you have not read.** Your prompt is an upload. Treat it like one.
>
> **DO** — *Return to the finished asset.*
>
> Our demonstration asset is finished, and it is good. Two questions before we publish it.
>
> **ASK** — What must we label? …
>
> **ASK** — And on which platform, under which rule? …
>
> If you cannot answer both, the asset is not finished. It is only *generated*.

## SLIDE 17 — Building a Repeatable Team Practice
**⏱ 41:00 → 43:00 · 2 min · ≈ 250 words**
**ON SCREEN:** Four artefacts as four labelled boxes. Nothing else on the slide.

**SAY**

> Everything so far is a *skill*. And a skill lives in one person's head — and it leaves the building when that person leaves. Here is how you turn it into a **capability** that belongs to your organisation. Four artefacts. None of them is expensive.
>
> **One: a written prompt template library.** Every prompt that produced a published asset goes into a shared file, with the tool name, the date, and the licence. Not a folder of pictures — a folder of **recipes**.
>
> **Two: an approved reference set.** The locked subject images, and the brand style anchor. Once these exist, a new colleague can produce an on-brand asset on their first afternoon — because the hard decisions were made once, and stored.
>
> **Three: a review step with a named person accountable.** Not "the team reviews it". **A name.** Automation changes who produces the work. It does not change who is accountable for it.
>
> **Four: a disclosure default.** Decide once, in advance, what you label and where — so that nobody has to make that judgement under deadline pressure, at four o'clock on a Friday, alone.
>
> One more note for the library, and it is a legal one. **The copyright position of artificially generated output varies by jurisdiction, and it is not settled.** Rights in training data are actively disputed. And generated images can accidentally contain a third-party logo, a trademarked character, or a recognisable building design.
>
> So record what you generated, with which tool, under which licence, on which date. That record is almost free to keep — and if it is ever questioned, it is extremely valuable.
>
> Four artefacts. A library. A reference set. A named approver. A disclosure default. That is the entire organisational work.
