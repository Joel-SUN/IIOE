What this workbook is

The course teaches a method in 47 minutes. A method is not a skill, and a skill is not acquired by watching. This workbook is the part where you do it. It contains eleven exercises built on the 47 short extracts and the 4 prompt panels that appear in the course, together with every prompt that produced them.

Part I needs no software, no account and no budget. You read a prompt, you write down what you expect to see, then you play the extract and compare. Part II needs a generation tool and follows the textile brief from the live demonstration, all the way to a publication sheet.

Before you start

  • Work through the exercises in order. Each one uses a term the previous one installed.
  • Write your prediction before you play the extract. The workbook enforces this: the clips stay locked until you have written something. This is not a formality — the gap between what you expected and what you got is the entire lesson.
  • Prompts are shown in English in both language versions of this workbook. Mainstream image and video models are most precise in English, so copy them as they are rather than translating them first. The surrounding instructions are in your language.
  • Film-grammar terms are always given trilingually — 中文 · English · Français — in Annex B. Use that table; do not guess a translation.
  • Keep a single working document open beside the workbook. Everything you write in Part II goes into it: the prompts, the drift tables, the approved still, the publication sheet.

Ground rules that apply to every exercise

  1. Do not generate any identifiable real person. If a human figure is needed, use a wholly fictional description and state, on the asset itself, that the person does not exist.
  2. Never let the model generate your logo, your packaging text or your legal copy. Image models render text and fine typography unreliably. Generate the picture, then composite real vector artwork and real type on top.
  3. No vendor name, product name or price may appear in any frame you record or publish from these exercises.
  4. Check that the tool’s terms permit commercial use before you generate anything you intend to publish. This is criterion one in Annex E and it is checked first, not last.
  5. Record the tool you used, its licence terms and the date you checked them. Terms change; your production file is the evidence of what was true when you generated.
  6. Label every generated asset you publish, on every platform you publish it to. Slide 18 of the course teaches this; TP 11 makes you do it.

Questions

How many separate instructions does the full prompt of item 1 contain? Count them one by one.

At least twelve: the duration, the fact that it is based on a supplied image, the camera movement type, the quality of that movement (slow, smooth), the gradual reveal, the characters’ position, three separate character actions, the wind, the background subject and its behaviour, the lighting, and the style and finish. If you counted fewer, you merged clauses that the model reads separately.

The prompt asks for “about ten seconds”. The clip runs 10.1 seconds. What is worth remembering about that?

Duration is one of the few numeric instructions a video model honours closely. State it explicitly and you get close to what you asked for. Most other numbers in a prompt — “three buoys”, “two windows” — are far less reliable.

Where in the prompt is the word “romantic” doing work, and where is the romance actually produced?

“Romantic” appears only once, at the very end, inside a style tag. The romance you see is produced by three concrete actions written earlier: he tucks a strand of hair behind her ear, they exchange a tender look, they both turn towards the sunset. The adjective sets the grade; the actions make the feeling. This is the abstract-to-concrete rule of Annex D in a single example.

Delete the sentence “The camera performs a slow, smooth dolly out, gradually revealing the romantic riverside setting.” What would you lose?

Two things, and they are different things. You lose the movement — the frame would sit still or drift at the model’s discretion. And you lose the reveal, which is a narrative event: the setting is withheld at the start and given at the end. That sentence is element four, the one most often omitted, and it is carrying both the camera and the storytelling.

The clip keeps the Eiffel Tower in frame. Why was that left in, when so much else in the course avoids naming real places?

Because it is genuinely visible in the clip. Removing it from the prompt would have broken the correspondence between the prompt and the result, and that correspondence is the whole teaching device of this workbook. A real public monument that appears in the frame is described honestly; what the course removes instead is any token that would fix a subject’s ethnicity by default.

What to keep from this exercise

Tip

Questions

Which of the six elements is never illustrated anywhere in this workbook, and why?

Element six, audio. All 47 extracts are muted in this workbook, not silent: each carries an audio track containing a low-frequency bed that the video model generated on its own, which no prompt asked for. Muting it keeps the clips running underneath the course narration without covering it. No extract can therefore demonstrate a score, an ambient sound or an effect that was chosen. This is a real gap and it is stated rather than papered over — if you need to evaluate audio prompting, you must generate it yourself in Part II.

Element four requires two things, not one. What are they?

The movement type, and the start and end shot size. “A slow dolly in” names a movement but leaves the model to choose where it begins and where it stops. “A slow dolly in, from a medium shot to a close-up of the hands” is executable.

A colleague writes “cinematic, high quality, 4K, professional”. Which element is that, and why is it weak?

Element five. It names no dominant, no contrast, no saturation, no depth of field and no film character, which are five separate instructions the model can each execute. “Cinematic” alone is too vague to be actionable, and “4K” describes a deliverable rather than a look. The consequence is the generic AI appearance: competent, and instantly recognisable as generated.

You strengthened the weakest element. Your result changed in a way you did not expect. What is the correct response?

Write down the unexpected change and keep the prompt. An unexpected change is information about the model, and models differ. The wrong response is to keep editing until you get the picture you had in your head: you will end up with a prompt you cannot explain, which means you cannot reuse it, which means your team cannot reuse it.

Questions

Are these four the same shot? Say precisely what is held constant and what varies.

Subject, action, framing and duration are held constant; only the grade varies. That is what makes the four comparable at all. The transferable rule: when you test style, change nothing else. If you change the grade and the camera move in the same generation and the result improves, you cannot say which of the two did it.

Which is riskier in a commercial asset: high saturation or low saturation? Justify from the business side, not the aesthetic side.

High saturation, usually, because it moves the product’s apparent colour away from the colour the customer will receive. A textile, a paint, a garment or a food item sold on a saturated image creates a return and a complaint. Low saturation risks looking dull; high saturation risks being wrong about the product. Where the colour of the product is the purchase decision, grade from a real photograph of the real product instead — that is the tier rule of TP 11.

Appendix B of the course calls one lighting phrase the single highest-value term available. Which one?

“Volumetric lighting” — lumière volumétrique — visible beams where the air carries mist, dust or smoke. One term noticeably lifts perceived production quality, because it implies a designed environment rather than a lit one. It is also cheap: it costs six words and no extra generation.

You wrote four one-line variants. A colleague reads them and cannot tell which product they are for. What is missing?

Elements one and three. A style line on its own is a grade, not a shot. In production you will paste that line at the end of a prompt that already carries the subject and the setting — which is exactly what a style anchor does in TP 7. Written alone, it must still be checked against the whole prompt before you judge it.

Questions

Which direction gives the strongest texture and volume, and what is its typical business use?

Lateral light — 侧光, lumière latérale. Half the frame in light and half in shade. Its business use is craftsmanship and material close-ups, which is precisely the textile brief of Part II: a hand-woven fabric photographed frontally looks flat and loses the weave.

Which light direction must be avoided in marketing, and which one is legitimately useful but rarely flattering?

Under light — 底光, lumière en contre-plongée — is abnormal and unsettling; avoid it in marketing. Top light — 顶光, lumière zénithale — drops the eye sockets into shadow and reads as oppressive, which makes it legitimate for fatigue and pressure and poor for almost everything else. Backlight is the opposite case: it underexposes a face but is exactly right for an aspirational brand film or a closing shot.

Colour must be written as three separate instructions. Which three, and why is one word not enough?

Dominant, contrast, saturation. They are independent: a frame can have a warm dominant with low contrast and high saturation, or a warm dominant with high contrast and low saturation, and these look completely different. A single word such as “cinematic” or “warm” leaves two of the three to the model, and the model resolves them to its own default — which is the generic look.

The course names four times of day. The extracts illustrate four too, but not the same four. What is the difference?

The course names golden hour, blue hour, solar noon and urban night. The extracts show daylight, night, sunrise and sunset. Sunrise and sunset are close to golden hour, and night is close to urban night, but neither pair is the same term, and blue hour and solar noon have no extract at all. Solar noon is the one you will most often need to refuse: hard top shadows and flattened saturation.

Your three-line specification for the office-lit product. What must it not contain?

Any word that describes how the picture should feel rather than where the light comes from. “Bright”, “clean”, “professional”, “appealing” and “high quality” all fail this test. What passes: “soft lateral key light from camera left through a large diffuser; cool white dominant at roughly 5000 K; medium contrast; saturation true to the physical sample; graduated shadow falling away to camera right.” The question to ask of every adjective is the one Annex D puts at the centre of the course: how would a camera capture this?

What these ten extracts do not cover: frontal light (顺光) has no dedicated extract, hard light has none, lateral light is not labelled as such even though it may appear inside another clip, volumetric light has none, and blue hour and solar noon have none. Do not present this page as illustrating every row of the light table, and do not let a learner leave believing it does.

The course names six shot sizes. How many do the extracts illustrate, and which two are missing?

Four: close-up, medium, full and wide. Extreme wide (大远景, plan d’ensemble) and extreme close-up (特写, très gros plan) have no extract. Both are the ones that carry the most commercial weight — extreme wide establishes the scale of an operation, extreme close-up is the craftsmanship shot — so this is a gap worth naming out loud rather than leaving a learner to discover it in production.

The course paragraph on shot types names point of view, over-the-shoulder and the insert. What does the third group on this page actually show instead?

It counts subjects in frame: single, two, group, establishing. That is a different axis entirely — how many people the shot contains rather than from where it is seen. It is a useful complement and it is not the illustration of that paragraph. Point of view, over-the-shoulder and shot/reverse shot appear later, in the transition group of TP 7, and the insert has no extract anywhere in this workbook.

Which composition term exists specifically for advertising, and what does it require you to do before you generate?

Negative space — espace négatif. It requires you to decide, before generating, where the headline or the logo will sit, and to ask for empty picture in that region. The alternative — generating a beautiful busy frame and then hunting for somewhere to put the type — always ends in a compromised layout or a regeneration. Reserve the space in the prompt.

You wrote two shot specifications for the workshop and the hands. Should they be generated as one prompt or two?

Two. One movement per shot is a standing rule, and the same logic applies to one framing per shot. A prompt that asks for the workshop and then the hands is asking for a cut, and cuts are written at the segmentation stage — TP 7 — not inside a single shot. Generate the two separately and hold them together with a style anchor.

Centred composition and symmetrical composition both look balanced. When is each the wrong choice?

Centred composition flattens a subject that has an interesting environment, because it denies the background any weight; it is right for an ID-style product portrait and wrong for a scene. Symmetry reads as formality and control, so it suits architecture and institutional framing and works against anything meant to feel spontaneous, handmade or human — which includes most of the textile brief.

Questions

The course names ten movements. These extracts cover all ten plus two additions. Which two?

Follow shot (plan de suivi) and rack focus (bascule de point). Present them as additions to the table, not as evidence that the table is incomplete. Rack focus is worth the addition: it moves attention without moving the camera, which is the only way to shift the viewer from an object to a face in a single locked-off shot.

What are the two standing rules for writing camera movement?

Always write the start and end shot size, and put only one movement per shot. The first rule is what turns a named movement into an executable one; the second is what stops a generation from becoming a compromise between two intentions, neither of which lands.

A dolly out and a crane up can both end on a wide shot of the same scene. How do you choose?

By what the move means, not by what it shows. A dolly out stays at the subject’s height and reveals environment, which reads as isolation or scale measured from the ground. A crane changes the viewpoint as it rises, moving from a person to a landscape, which reads as epic scale or as the story stepping back. If you need the viewer to feel the subject is small, dolly out. If you need them to feel the operation is large, crane up.

Handheld produces tension. Name one business asset where that is the right choice and one where it is definitely the wrong choice.

Right: a behind-the-scenes or founder-story piece, where instability reads as presence and honesty. Wrong: a catalogue product image or any asset whose job is to establish that the product is reliable and the business is in control — a moving frame implies a moving camera operator, and that is the opposite of the message. It is also wrong on a small phone screen, where the shake reads as a playback fault.

Which movement in Annex B expresses “three months passed” in a single sentence, and why is it not in this exercise?

The temporal jump cut — 时间跳切, jump cut temporel. The camera stays completely locked and only posture and light change, day to dusk to night. It is not here because it is a segmentation device rather than a camera movement: nothing moves. It is called the most overlooked and best-value transition in the course, and you will meet it in TP 7.

Questions

Method C is an anchor, not a shot. What exactly does it hold constant, and what does it deliberately leave alone?

It holds element five constant — grade, colour temperature, lens character, film stock, depth of field — so that shots generated separately, possibly days apart and possibly on different models, read as one film. It deliberately leaves the subject, the action, the setting and the camera alone, because those are what differs between shots. An anchor that also fixes the subject is not an anchor, it is a template, and it produces seven nearly identical shots.

The anchor shown in method C is warm. The anchor read aloud on slide 15 of the course is cold grey-blue. Which one is correct?

Both. They are two different brands. The value of the panel is the form an anchor takes, not the palette it happens to carry — if the two had matched, a learner would have gone away believing that an anchor is a specific look rather than a reusable sentence. The cold grey-blue anchor belongs to the Brittany fisherman example the course uses throughout; the warm one belongs to whatever clip it was written for.

Method B uses timecodes. When is that worth the extra effort over method A?

When the shots have to land on a beat, on a spoken line, or on a fixed total duration — an advertisement cut to music, a title sequence, anything with a hard delivery length. Timecodes also help when several people generate different shots of the same film, because they remove the argument about how long each shot is. Method A is faster and is right for most internal and social content, where nothing has to hit a mark.

Which transition is called the most overlooked and the best value in the course, and what does it cost you to produce?

The temporal jump cut. Almost nothing: the camera is completely locked, so there is no movement to specify and no start and end shot size to get wrong. The rack focus appears here as item 50 and again in TP 6 as item 41. What is the difference between the two uses?

In TP 6 it is presented as a camera technique — attention moves without the camera moving. Same mechanism, two different jobs. The transition table in Annex B lists it as “focus pull”, which is why the two labels differ.

Objective. Learn what consistency actually depends on, by removing it deliberately and watching what happens.

This exercise produces bad assets on purpose. That is the point. Steps

What you are likely to find

The synonym changed almost nothing. Is the exercise broken?

No, and this is the more interesting outcome. Models are increasingly robust to a single synonym in a long, well-specified prompt, because the rest of the description carries the subject. The finding to record is that consistency depends on the whole description rather than on any one word — which means the dangerous edit is not a synonym, it is a deletion. The anchor deletion changed the picture but the product still looks the same. What has actually broken?

The film, not the product. One shot can survive losing its anchor; a sequence cannot. The anchor’s job is to make separately generated shots read as one work, so its loss only becomes visible when you play two shots back to back. Run break-anchor on both shot-v1 and shot-v3 from TP 9 and play them together — that is where the damage shows.

Restoring both did not restore the result. What does that mean?

That the model is not deterministic at the prompt level, so a prompt is a specification with a tolerance rather than a recipe. Two consequences for a business: you must keep the generated asset that was approved, not just the prompt that produced it, and you must not promise a colleague that re-running the prompt will reproduce the frame. Some tools expose a seed for exactly this reason; if yours does, record it alongside the prompt.

What is the rule most teams end up writing?

Some version of: the subject description and the style anchor are copied and pasted verbatim, never retyped, and never paraphrased — including in translation. If a phrase needs to change, one person changes it in the single source document and everyone re-copies. The reason is not aesthetic; it is that retyping is where a synonym, a comma or a dropped clause enters, and none of those is visible in the prompt when you are reading it back three weeks later.

Annex C

Annex C — The six-element card

Print this page. It is designed to sit beside the keyboard while you write a prompt, and it is the checklist TP 8 asks you to verify script-v1 against. Six boxes, and a box that is empty is a decision you made, not an accident.

Warning

Four things that must never be in the box: a model-generated logo or packaging text, a price, a vendor name, and any description of an identifiable real person.

Before you press generate

  • Read the prompt aloud. Any clause you stumble over, the model will stumble over.
  • For every adjective, ask: how would a camera capture this? If there is no answer, replace the adjective with an action, a light position or a composition.
  • One line of dialogue maximum, and no more than eight to ten words in it. Beyond that, lip-sync failure rises sharply. If you can, use voice over a non-speaking subject instead.
  • If dialogue is unavoidable, use a close-up. Lip-sync error is far less visible in a small frame than in a wide shot.

Annex E

Annex E — Choosing a tool, and the safeguards

This workbook is tool-independent and every exercise in it works on any current model. Part II nonetheless requires you to pick one, and the course evaluates candidates against five criteria, in this order. The order is the point: most business users check the last two first and discover the first one after they have already published.

  1. Commercial-use licensing of the output. A hard requirement, checked first. This is also the requirement most often overlooked by business users choosing a tool for their own campaigns. Check the terms, not the marketing page, and check whether the free tier carries the same rights as the paid one — it frequently does not.
  2. Vendor lock-in. Prefer open, transferable approaches. This is the standing selection principle of the course: a prompt library you cannot move to another tool is an asset that depreciates the moment the vendor changes its pricing.
  3. Image-to-video quality and consistency. TP 9 depends on holding a subject stable across shots, so this is the criterion that decides whether the workflow works at all.
  4. Free or low-cost quota — enough for a whole class generating at the same time. Part II needs roughly twenty generations per learner.
  5. Interface language. Learners are not native English or French speakers and have no technical background, so an interface in a language they read slowly costs real time in a supervised session.
Tip

Record the chosen tool, its licence terms and the date of verification in the production file, so that the compliance position can be re-checked later. TP 11 turns this into a line of the publication sheet.

Safeguards for a supervised session

  • Prepare an offline screen recording of every demonstration step. Network failure, quota exhaustion and rate limiting all happen mid-session, and a recording means the session survives them.
  • No vendor branding, product name or pricing may be visible in any recorded frame.
  • Verify that the tool’s terms permit commercial use before the session, not during it.
  • Do not generate any identifiable real person. If a human figure is required, use a wholly fictional description and state on screen that the person does not exist.
  • Test at 1080p and confirm legibility after encoding. A prompt that looks right at full resolution can become unreadable on a phone once compressed.
  • Every generated asset carries an AI-generation notice, because the course teaches disclosure and the workbook has to model it.
